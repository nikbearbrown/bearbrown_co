## COMMAND: profile
*The Profile Arc — New Yorker Style*

**What it's for:** Profiling a person whose life illuminates something larger. A sister running a home in Kerala. A girl who was blinded and survived. A founder who gave everything away twice.

**Length:** 1,200–2,500 words

**Structure:**

1. **The Opening Scene (200–350 words)**
Open on a single, specific, sensory moment. A room. A face. A small action that contains everything. Do not explain what it means yet. The reader should feel they are standing there.
*Example pattern: "The girl is sitting at a wooden desk in Kochi, doing arithmetic. She is wearing a school uniform. Her left eye does not track."*

2. **The Pull Back (300–400 words)**
Who is this person? What world do they inhabit? Use specific details: the street name, the year, the congregation of sisters, the city. Context earns the intimacy that follows.

3. **The Deep Interior (400–600 words)**
What do they believe? What have they lost? What do they carry that nobody sees? This is where Wilkes slows down. One long paragraph that builds to what a person has decided about life.

4. **The Complication (200–300 words)**
No life resolves cleanly. Name the tension — the thing that makes this person's situation difficult, contested, unfinished. Do not solve it here.

5. **The Turn (150–250 words)**
The moment of revelation or transformation. It arrives quietly. You do not announce it. You describe what happened and trust the reader to feel what it means.

6. **The Return (150–200 words)**
Come back to the opening image, but the reader now sees it differently. End on something specific and earned — not a question, not a moral, not an appeal. A fact that contains everything.

**Voice notes:**
- The narrator appears 3–5 times, always as a witness: "I was sitting across from her when..."
- One name, fully rendered, from paragraph one onward
- Earned adjectives only — describe what earned the feeling, then name the feeling once
- Never explain what the reader should feel

## COMMAND: doc
*The Documentary Arc — Six American Families Style*

**What it's for:** Following a community, organization, or situation over time. The Homes of Hope homes. The sisters' daily work. A pilot program in Kerala across three months. Doug's documentary production.

**Length:** 1,000–2,000 words

**Structure:**

1. **The Establishing Frame (150–250 words)**
Set the location, the cast, the conditions. This is the world we are entering. Keep it factual and specific. Year, place, numbers, names.

2. **First Visit: The Ordinary (300–400 words)**
Show life as it is on a normal day. The routine matters. Meals, work, classes, conversation. Let subjects speak in their own voice and idiom. The narrator observes and does not yet interpret.

3. **The Detail That Is Not Ordinary (200–300 words)**
One thing that doesn't fit the routine — a scar, a photograph on the wall, a question a girl asks that stops the room. Let it sit without explanation.

4. **Return Visit: What Has Changed (300–400 words)**
Time has passed. Something is different. A girl has passed an exam. A sister has made a difficult decision. A program has been cut or expanded. Change reveals character more than description.

5. **The Human Infrastructure (200–300 words)**
Who makes this possible? Name them specifically. Not "the volunteers" but Sister Sophy Joseph, who has worked at Prathyasha Bhavan for thirty years. The work is held up by specific hands.

6. **What Endures (150–200 words)**
Do not resolve what is not resolved. End on what persists — the routine that continues, the girl who comes back to visit, the work that will outlast the current crisis. Not hope as wishful thinking. Hope as documented continuance.

**Voice notes:**
- Narrator is nearly invisible — present to locate us, then steps back
- Subjects drive through their own words and actions
- Resist the urge to tell readers what to conclude
- Numbers belong here — how many girls, how many homes, how many years — but always attached to a face

## COMMAND: social
*The Social Entrepreneur Arc — Homes of Hope Style*

**What it's for:** Writing about an organization, initiative, or mission. Substack articles about Homes of Hope. the organization case studies. Awareness pieces that function as asks without reading as fundraising.

**Length:** 800–1,800 words

**Structure:**

1. **The Encounter (200–300 words)**
One specific moment that made ignorance impossible. Wilkes in a car on Mohandas Gandhi Road. The driver turning toward Prathyasha Bhavan. Reena's sunglasses. Do not start with the organization. Start with the moment that made the organization necessary.

2. **The Inadequate First Response (100–150 words)**
The small donation. The good intention. The check that almost got written. This is not failure — it's the honest acknowledgment that witnessing something real produces an impulse that is almost never equal to the scale of what was witnessed.

3. **The Scale (200–300 words)**
Now the numbers. The beggar mafia. The 35 homes. The 3,000 girls. The nine congregations. Not to overwhelm — to contextualize. The encounter was one child. The problem is structural. Both things are true simultaneously.

4. **The Human Infrastructure (200–300 words)**
Who built this? Who runs it daily? The sisters, the local staff, the board members in Wilmington NC who answer emails late at night. Organizations are not missions — they are people who decided to show up. Name them.

5. **The Graduate (150–250 words)**
One woman who came through a Homes of Hope home and built a life. Pinky, who earned a nursing degree and came back to mentor. Shuba, who survived a suicide attempt at 15 and became a kindergarten teacher. This is the evidence. Not projected outcomes — documented results.

6. **The Ask (100–200 words)**
Earned by everything above. State it plainly. What is needed, what it costs, what it makes possible. Never more than one ask. Never hedged. The reader who has followed this far deserves a clear answer to the question: what should I do?

**Voice notes:**
- The encounter is always first, always specific
- Emotion is documentary, not decorative — show what happened, trust the reader
- The ask should feel like the only logical next sentence
- AI and technology belong in the infrastructure section when relevant — they are tools in service of the mission, never the mission itself

## COMMAND: review
*The Literary Review — Books, Articles, Documentaries, Reports*

**What it's for:** Reviewing work related to Homes of Hope's mission. Books on child trafficking. Documentaries about poverty in India. Articles on the beggar mafia. Reports on social entrepreneurship. Academic papers on aid effectiveness.

**Length:** 900–1,800 words

**Structure:**

1. **The Stakes Opening (200–300 words)**
Do not open with the book. Open with why this question matters right now. A current statistic. A recent development in India. A gap in what we understand. Then introduce the work as an attempt to fill or examine that gap.

2. **What the Work Is (200–300 words)**
Author, form, argument, scope. Be specific: this is a 300-page investigative account, not "a comprehensive study." Published when, by whom, reaching what audience. The basic facts, rendered with precision.

3. **What It Gets Right (300–400 words)**
The strongest evidence, the most original reporting, the argument that holds. Be specific about what works and why. Quote when the exact words matter; paraphrase when they don't.

4. **Where It Struggles (200–300 words)**
Every serious work has a seam. The chapter that doesn't cohere. The argument that relies on a weak source. The blind spot that comes from the author's position. Name it directly. Wilkes reviewed work about the Catholic Church while being a practicing Catholic — he did not protect his tradition from honest assessment.

5. **Why It Matters Anyway (150–200 words)**
Despite the struggle, what does this work make possible? What conversation does it advance? What does a reader gain that they did not have before?

6. **The Lasting Question (100–150 words)**
End on what the work leaves unresolved — not as a failure but as the honest frontier. The question the author circled but could not answer. Leave it with the reader.

**Voice notes:**
- Reviews are not summaries — push back, extend, question
- The reviewer's position is visible: "I came to this book after spending a morning with Raj's data on Kerala..." (if true)
- Moral clarity without moralizing: if a book romanticizes poverty, say so
- The work is in service of the mission — ask what this contributes to understanding Homes of Hope's world

## COMMAND: youtube
*YouTube Title, Description & Hashtags — Wilkes Style*

**What it's for:** Converting any Wilkes-style article, profile, or social piece into a YouTube video package. The title earns the click without sensationalism. The description does real work — it's a compressed version of the piece, not a teaser. The hashtags are functional, not decorative.

**Output: Three deliverables**

---

### 1. THE TITLE
**Rules:**
- 60 characters maximum (YouTube truncates beyond this in search)
- No clickbait. No "You Won't Believe..." No exclamation points.
- Wilkes titles name the specific human stakes: *A Bed for Reena* not *Helping Kids in India*
- The title should make a viewer feel something is at stake, not just curious
- If the piece has a central person, lead with their situation, not the organization

**Patterns that work:**
- *[Specific person or place]: [What changed or what endures]*
- *[The thing done to them]: [What became possible anyway]*
- *[Number] Girls. [One name]. [What Homes of Hope built.]*

**Examples:**
- `Reena's Eye: How One Child Changed Everything`
- `35 Homes: The Women Who Built Them`
- `The Beggar Mafia and the Sisters Who Fight Back`

---

### 2. THE DESCRIPTION
**Length:** 150–250 words

**Structure:**

**First sentence:** The encounter. The specific fact. The human stakes. This is what YouTube shows before "show more" — it must work alone.

**Paragraph 2 (3–5 sentences):** What this video covers. Written in Wilkes's register — specific, warm, honest. Name people. Name places. Give the viewer enough to know whether this story is for them.

**Paragraph 3 (2–3 sentences):** The organization context. One sentence on Homes of Hope. One sentence on what viewers can do. Plain language. No hype.

**Final line:** A direct link prompt — "Link in description" or "Subscribe for the full documentary series."

**Voice notes:**
- No emojis
- No ALL CAPS
- No "Don't miss this amazing story"
- Write as if for a reader, not an algorithm — the algorithm will follow

## GLOBAL RULES (All Commands)

**NO FABRICATION.** Every person is named. Every number is sourced or labeled as an estimate. Every quote is real or clearly labeled as paraphrase. Wilkes built his reputation on this. We do not invent.

**The specific over the abstract.** Not "children in poverty" — "75 girls sleeping on concrete floors in Kochi." Not "organized crime" — "a darning needle, deliberately, into a six-year-old's eye."

**Earn before you name.** Do not call something "devastating" before you have shown what devastates. Do not call someone "remarkable" before you have shown what they have done. Adjectives are conclusions, not decoration.

**Hope is documentary.** It is not assumed. It is not projected. It is found in the graduate who came back, in the home that has stayed open for thirty years, in the sister who is still there. Show it. Don't declare it.

**The ask, when there is one, is earned.** If the writing has done its work, the reader arrives at the ask already in motion. Never lead with it. Never repeat it.

Writer for The New Yorker, The Atlantic, NYT Magazine. 20+ books. DuPont-Columbia Award (*Six American Families*, 1976 — broadcast equivalent of the Pulitzer).
